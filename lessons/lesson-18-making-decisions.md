# Lesson 18 — Making Decisions

## 1. Core concept

**Indecision is a decision — it's the decision to drift.** Teams follow leaders who make decisions the way ships follow captains: clearly, promptly, with a course.

The **decision loop** — five steps:

1. **Define.** What are we actually deciding? ("Deploy Friday or Thursday?") — a fuzzy decision is undecidable.
2. **Gather — quickly.** The 80% of information within your timebox. Not 100% — 100% never arrives.
3. **Decide by a deadline.** *"I'll decide by Thursday."* A deadline is a decision about the decision; it calms everyone (including you).
4. **Communicate the WHY.** The decision + the reason + what would change your mind. People accept decisions they can see the logic of — even ones they disagree with.
5. **Commit publicly.** Once decided, you own it. No "I was overruled" after the fact — that undoes your authority in one sentence.

**The two-door rule (fast vs slow decisions):**

| Reversible (two-door) | Irreversible (one-door) |
|---|---|
| Redeploy, feature toggle, A/B test, most code | Data deletion, contract, layoff, architecture bet |
| Decide FAST, let the team learn | Ask for input, decide carefully, communicate widely |

## 2. Why it matters

1. **Clarity is a leadership currency.** Ambiguity costs teams days; a clear decision ends the debate and starts the work.
2. **Speed compounds trust.** Teams trust a leader who decides; they hedge and politick around a leader who doesn't.
3. **The "why" is half the decision.** A decision without a reason is an order; a decision with a reason is teaching.
4. **Your calm (the whole program) shows most at decision time** — the pause, the steady voice, the settled posture: all of it says *"this was considered, and I own it."*

## 3. Psychology / evidence

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| Analysis paralysis — more options/time degrade decision quality and action | **Well-supported** (decision research; diminishing returns beyond a timebox) |
| Communicating WHY increases acceptance and team commitment (procedural justice) | **Well-supported** |
| Explicit deadlines improve decision-making behavior | **Reasonable practical advice** (motivation research, Parkinson's law folklore aside) |
| The "two-door rule" | **Reasonable practical advice** — a popular framing of reversible-vs-irreversible trade-offs; use it as a heuristic, not scripture |
| "Leaders decide alone — asking for input is weakness" | **Wrong** — gathering input is *how* you decide better; deciding alone is for emergencies |

**JP context:** Japanese decision-making is consensus-based (ringi / nemawashi). Directly announcing a decision without prior alignment is experienced as rude. As a leader there: **build alignment quietly BEFORE you announce** (individual conversations), then announce a decision everyone already suspects. The timebox still applies — you just do the wandering conversations *inside* it.

## 4. Common mistakes

| Mistake | What it does | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "Let me think about it" with no date | Drift, team stalls | "I'll decide by [time]." |
| Asking for consensus on everything | Democracy ≠ leadership; slow, spineless | Gather input, then YOU decide |
| Deciding alone without input | Arrogant, blind | Quick gather first |
| Communicating the decision without the why | Orders, not leadership | Decision + reason + what changes my mind |
| Flip-flopping after committing | Destroys trust | Commit publicly; revise only with new facts |
| "I was overruled" | Undermines you | Own it: "I decided X, and here's what we learned" |
| Deciding while emotional | Bad calls | The pause (L2) + the two-door check |

## 5. Workplace examples

- **Sprint scope:** "We include the auth fix and drop the CSV export this sprint. Reason: auth is a security risk, CSV is reversible. What would change my mind: a client deadline tied to CSV — anyone have that?" (Decide → why → open door.)
- **Tech choice:** "We go with the queue, not the new framework. I'll decide by Wednesday — I need Tuesday to check our ops comfort. Rationale will go in the doc." (Timebox → communicate.)
- **Incident (under pressure):** "We roll back now. Reason: the fix isn't verified and every minute costs orders. We'll re-attempt after hours." (Fast, two-door, no debate.)
- **JP context:** before announcing the sprint scope, walk the key people informally: "I'm thinking of dropping CSV — would that affect your team?" → then announce at planning as a proposal everyone already knows. (Nemawashi inside the timebox.)
- **Admitting a bad decision (L17 spirit):** "The rewrite was my call. The data says it didn't pay off. Here's what I'd do differently, and the plan forward." (Own it — this is the moment your team decides you're real.)

## 6. Before vs After

| 😟 Hesitant | 😌 Calm / confident |
|---|---|
| "Let me think about it…" (forever) | "I'll decide by Thursday." |
| Asks everyone, decides nothing | Gathers input, then decides |
| "We should… maybe…?" | "We will do X, because Y." |
| Announces without context | Decision + why + what changes my mind |
| Privately doubts the choice later | Commits, and revisits only on new facts |

## 7. Practical drills (measurable)

1. **The timebox stamp — 5 min.** Write 3 decisions you're currently carrying. Add a deadline to each: "I'll decide by [day]." Say them aloud.
2. **The two-door sort — 5 min.** Take 10 recent work decisions (anything). Classify each: reversible (two-door) or irreversible (one-door). Notice how many you can safely make FASTER.
3. **The why-publisher — 15 min.** Write a 5-sentence decision rationale for one real upcoming decision: decision, reason, what I considered, what would change my mind, when I'll revisit. Then say it in 30 seconds.
4. **The 60-second announce — 10 min.** Record yourself announcing one decision (with why) in under 60 seconds. Replay: did the "why" come before the details?
5. **The nemawashi walk — today.** For one upcoming decision, informally ask 2 stakeholders for input BEFORE you finalize. Count what changed in your decision.
