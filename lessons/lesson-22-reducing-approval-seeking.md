# Lesson 22 — Reducing Approval-Seeking Behavior

> Program: Leadership Presence & Body Language · Phase 5 — Authenticity (2/5)
> Delivered: 2026-09-22 · Builds on: L21 (genuine confidence), L15 (disagreeing), L18 (decisions), L14 (speaking clearly)

---

## 1. Core concept

**Approval-seeking is outsourcing your worth to other people's reactions.** It sounds like: *"Is that okay?"* after your own decision, *"We could maybe… right?"* before you even believe the idea, or saying yes because saying no would make someone frown.

The leader's counter-move is to **seek alignment, not approval.**
- **Approval =** "Do you accept me?" (about your worth)
- **Alignment =** "Do we agree on the plan?" (about the work)

They sound similar; they are different planets. Approval is emotional fuel you beg for. Alignment is a working agreement you co-build. Politeness stays; the *need* goes.

Genuine confidence (L21) is the engine. Approval-seeking is the leak. You can fill the tank all week and lose it in one "please like me" sentence.

## 2. Why it matters

1. **It erases your judgment.** If your answer depends on the last frown in the room, your technical opinion is not yours anymore.
2. **It invites bad scope (L18).** You accept every request because refusing is uncomfortable. The team's roadmap becomes whoever asked first.
3. **People can smell the need.** Needing approval lowers trust more than being wrong does.
4. **The team learns your price.** Once they see you bend under pressure, every estimate gets pushed.
5. **In JP culture it is dangerously easy to confuse.** Politeness, *keigo*, and indirect phrasing are cultural respect — not the same as the inner need. You must separate *the shell* (keep it) from *the need* (drop it).

## 3. Psychology / evidence

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| Humans are wired to want social acceptance | **Well-supported** (social psychology — need to belong) |
| Chronic approval-seeking correlates with anxiety and lower assertiveness | **Reasonable practical advice** (clinical observation; correlation studies) |
| People notice and judge you less than you fear (spotlight effect) | **Well-supported** (Gilovich et al.) |
| Leaders who are too agreeable are often rated as less competent | **Reasonable** (research on the warmth/competence trade-off; context matters) |
| Fear of negative evaluation drives over-apologizing and hedging | **Well-supported** (FNE literature) |
| "Always disagree loudly to show strength" | **Wrong for JP + wrong for trust** — genuine confidence is quiet (L21) |

**Key insight:** the opposite of approval-seeking is not rudeness. It is **being willing to be disagreed with.** You can be warm, polite, and respectful — and also say no, state a view, and survive a frown.

## 4. Common mistakes

| Mistake | What it does | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Turning statements into questions ("maybe… right? …?") | Gives your power away before you finish | Say it as a statement; then invite questions |
| Asking permission for your own decisions (L18) | You announce, not decide | Timebox, decide, explain WHY — then ask for input, not permission |
| Over-apologizing ("sorry" ×3 per sentence) | Reads as incompetence | Apologize once, with a fix |
| Changing your estimate under pressure | You train them to push | Hold your number; explain the cost (L15) |
| Reacting to every nod and frown mid-answer | You steer by weather | Finish your thought, then check understanding |
| Confusing JP politeness with approval | You over-soften into fog | Soft shell, same substance (L15) |

## 5. Workplace examples

- **A technical choice of yours:** *"I'm using the outbox pattern for the payment events. Here's why, and here's the trade-off. If anyone sees a problem, speak now — otherwise this is the design."* You decide; you invite challenge. That is leadership.
- **Scope push from a stakeholder:** *"I hear it's important. Adding it now cuts the login fix — I'd keep the login fix. If you need both, we move the date, and here's the new date."* Refuse the request, accept the discussion.
- **Disagreement in a review (L15):** *"I see it differently — here's my reasoning. I could be wrong; let's check the failure case."* Zero begging, zero attacking.
- **The manager asks "why did you do it this way?"** — a test. Do not apologize for the decision. *"Because X, and I'd do it again unless you see a problem with X."*
- **JP context (colleague's bad idea):** *"That could work. There's a risk I want to check though: the retry would hit the same queue. Could we look at that before we commit?"* Respectful shell, real substance — no need to please.

## 6. Before vs After

| 😟 Approval-seeking | 😌 Seeking alignment |
|---|---|
| "Is this okay…? Sorry, maybe I should…" | "Here's my recommendation and why." |
| Waits for a nod before trusting himself | Decides, explains, invites challenge |
| "Yes" under pressure | Holds the number, shows the cost |
| Changes course at the first frown | Finishes the thought, then checks |
| Over-apologizes | One apology + a fix |
| Mistakes politeness for the need | Keeps the shell, drops the need |

## 7. Practical drills (measurable)

1. **Statement intonation — 5 minutes.** Read 5 work sentences aloud, dropping the question rise at the end: *"We should move the deploy to Thursday."* (down, not up). Feel the difference. Do 3 sentences you'll actually use.
2. **The no-apology tour — 10 minutes.** Tell a story about your day WITHOUT "sorry", "maybe", "I think…?", "if that's okay". If you slip, restart the sentence. Record it once and listen.
3. **The 3-second pause before yes — all week.** Before any yes, count 3 silently. Ask: *do I want this, or do I want them to like me?* Refuse at least one thing this week, with a reason.
4. **The opinion-holding drill — one discussion.** In one meeting, hold one view for 5 full minutes while someone disagrees. You do not change, you do not chase — you explain, then stop.
5. **The permission audit — 1 minute/day.** Every time you catch "can I…?", "is it okay if…?" about YOUR OWN work — log it. Awareness is the first seal on the leak.

## 8. Real-world challenge (this week)

> Make **one decision** and announce it without asking permission: *"I'm doing X, because Y. I'll show the result Friday."*
> If a request arrives that you can't take, decline plainly: *"I can't — I'm already at capacity. Here's what I'd cut to make room."*
> Report back: the decision and the reaction.

## 10. Coaching exercise — ✋ STOP HERE

Record **90 seconds**: *"Tell me about a project decision you made. What did you choose, and why?"*

Then answer **3 questions**:
1. Count your "maybe / right? / sorry / I think…?" — how many times did you give your power away?
2. Did you explain your reasons like a decision-maker, or like someone asking to be allowed?
3. Rate your **approval-independence** 1–5.
