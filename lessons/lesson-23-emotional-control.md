# Lesson 23 — Emotional Control

> Program: Leadership Presence & Body Language · Phase 5 — Authenticity (3/5)
> Delivered: 2026-09-22 · Builds on: L2 (breathing), L3 (calm voice), L10 (silence), L19 (pressure), L17 (criticism)

---

## 1. Core concept

**Emotional control is not having no emotions. It is choosing what happens after the feeling arrives.**

The chain is: **stimulus → feeling → (the gap) → response.** Almost everyone is well-trained in the first two links — the trigger and the flash of anger, fear, or panic. Control lives in the third link: **the gap**, where you choose.

Two ideas make the gap possible:

1. **Regulate, don't suppress.** Suppressing = pushing the feeling down; it always leaks out later (voice, face, posture, a sharper word). Regulating = *feeling it, naming it, and steering what it does next.* The feeling is allowed; the reaction is chosen.
2. **The 90-second wave.** The chemical part of an emotion (adrenaline, cortisol) rises and falls in roughly 90 seconds. If you are still angry after 90 seconds, you are usually *re-triggering* it with thoughts ("he always does this…"). The thought loop — not the chemistry — is what you control.

Leaders are the weather of the room. Emotional contagion is real: if you stay calm, the team stays calm. If you panic, the panic spreads (L19 used this in incidents).

## 2. Why it matters

1. **Your team reads you as the forecast.** In an incident, your voice is the team's stability point. One panicked sentence undoes ten calm ones.
2. **One loss of control costs more trust than ten good meetings build.** People remember the day the leader shouted or froze.
3. **It protects the other tools.** Emotional control is the floor under L15 (disagreeing), L16 (feedback), L19 (pressure), L22 (approval-independence). Without it, technique collapses on the first trigger.
4. **In JP enterprise environments, emotional control IS professionalism.** Displaying strong emotion is culturally read as loss of face — yours and the room's. The calm leader is the senior leader.

## 3. Psychology / evidence

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| Emotions spread between people (contagion) | **Well-supported** (Hatfield, emotional contagion research) |
| Reframing an emotion (reappraisal) works better long-term than suppressing it | **Well-supported** (Gross's emotion-regulation research; suppression increases stress markers) |
| Labeling a feeling reduces its intensity ("name it to tame it") | **Reasonable–well-supported** (affect labeling, Lieberman) |
| The physiological sigh (double inhale + long exhale) quickly calms the body | **Well-supported** (respiratory physiology — fastest known calming breath) |
| The "90-second chemical wave" | **Reasonable/popular** (Jill Bolte Taylor's framing; the underlying rise-and-fall of the stress hormones is well-supported, the exact 90s is approximate) |
| Calling anxiety "excitement" improves performance | **Reasonable** (cognitive-reframing studies with mixed replication) |
| "Leaders should never show emotion" | **Wrong** — controlled emotion builds trust; numb leaders build distance |

## 4. Common mistakes

| Mistake | What it does | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Suppressing (bottling it) | Leaks sideways later | Feel it, name it, then choose |
| Reacting inside the first 90 seconds | The primal wave decides for you | Pause + one breath before responding (L10) |
| Letting one bad moment color the whole meeting | One spark, whole fire | Contain it to one topic; park the rest |
| Anger as a leadership tool | Fear-based control, kills trust | Calm directness, never volume |
| Numbness ("I don't care") | Reads as absent, not controlled | Allow the feeling; show the choice |

## 5. Workplace examples

- **Production incident, VP on the call (L19):** panic rises. You do one **physiological sigh** (short inhale, short inhale, long exhale through the mouth — 3–4 seconds). Then: *"Here's what we know, here's what we don't, here's what I'm doing."* The room's panic drops with your breathing.
- **A teammate's mistake cost you a night of work:** the flash of anger arrives. You count one breath before the sentence: *"Let's look at what happened so it doesn't repeat. Before that — take a breath, this is fixable."* (L17, L20.)
- **The manager is wrong in a review (L15):** you feel the urge to push back hard. You name it silently ("this is me defending my idea"), then respond with facts + the alternative, not the emotion.
- **A hard question in an interview:** the fear hits. You use the pause (L10), then *"Good question — let me think for a second."* Your voice stays steady because your body got a beat first (L3).
- **JP context (silence after bad news):** the room goes quiet and everyone looks at you. You stay in the silence (L10/L24), then state the next step calmly. That moment is where the team decides you are the leader.

## 6. Before vs After

| 😟 Reacting | 😌 Regulating |
|---|---|
| Speaks in the first 90 seconds | Pauses, sighs, then speaks |
| "I'm fine" (bottling) → explodes later | Names it: "I'm frustrated — one moment." |
| Panic voice: fast, high, loud | Steady, slower, quieter (L3) |
| One bad moment ruins the meeting | Contains the moment, moves on |
| Anger = control | Calm = control |
| Numb | Felt and chosen |

## 7. Practical drills (measurable)

1. **The physiological sigh — twice a day + before any hard call.** Breathe in through the nose twice (short, short), then one long slow exhale through the mouth. 3–4 seconds out. Two reps. This is the fastest physical calm lever you have.
2. **The 90-second pause — drill it once a day.** When something triggers you, do NOT react. Name the feeling in your head ("that's anger at being corrected"), and run the 90 seconds silently (count to 90 or breathe 8 times). Then choose. Log what you chose.
3. **The reframe lab — 5 minutes.** Take one thing that annoyed you recently. Write 3 different interpretations of it: (a) the worst, (b) the neutral ("I don't know his intent"), (c) the generous ("he's stressed too"). Say the neutral one out loud once. Reappraisal is a muscle.
4. **The naming drill — all week.** Every time you feel a strong emotion at work, say it silently in one word: "frustrated", "nervous", "disappointed". Naming short-circuits the thought loop.
5. **The thermometer voice drill — 3 minutes.** Record yourself reading one paragraph (any work text) three times: angry-fast, panicked-fast, then calm-slow. Compare. Calm is the one people would follow.

## 10. Coaching exercise — ✋ STOP HERE

Record **90 seconds**: *"Tell me about a time you lost your cool at work — what happened, what you felt, and what you'd do differently now."*

Then answer **3 questions**:
1. When you told the story, did your voice speed up or rise (a sign the feeling is still live) or stay steady?
2. Could you name the feeling in one word at the time?
3. Rate your **emotional control** 1–5 right now.
