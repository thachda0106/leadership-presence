# Lesson 7 — Personal Space

> Program: Leadership Presence & Body Language · Phase 2 — Connection (3/4)
> Delivered: 2026-09-19 · Builds on: Phase 1 (Posture, Breathing, Voice, Walking) + Eye Contact, Smile

## 1. Core concept

**Distance is a silent sentence.** Before you say a word, your physical distance has already said: *"I'm comfortable with you"*, *"I'm in charge here"*, or *"I'm crowding you."*

The classic map (Edward T. Hall's zones — a framework, not a law):

| Zone | Distance | What it's for |
|---|---|---|
| Intimate | 0 – 45 cm | Family, close friends, whispers |
| Personal | 45 cm – 1.2 m | 1-on-1 conversations, trusted colleagues |
| Social | 1.2 – 3.6 m | Meetings, strangers, formal talk |
| Public | 3.6 m+ | Speaking to a group, presentations |

**Mental model:** every person wears an invisible bubble. The bubble is *elastic* — it shrinks for people we trust and expands under stress or for strangers. A calm leader **reads the bubble and doesn't push it.** When you stay out of someone's bubble, they relax; when you enter it uninvited, their brain fires a tiny alarm.

Two practical rules:

1. **The two-step rule.** In any work conversation, default to about **an arm's length + a little more** (≈ 1.2 m, personal-to-social border). Two steps is a safe, respectful default. Adjust inward only when the other person clearly invites it (they lean in, they move closer).
2. **When in doubt, move back — never forward.** If you sense hesitation, the other person stepping back, or answers getting shorter, you are probably in the bubble. Give space; the conversation will improve.

## 2. Why it matters

1. **Comfort → trust.** People can't think clearly when their space is invaded. A teammate giving you bad news needs room to speak, not pressure.
2. **Pressure is a weapon — and it backfires.** Hovering over a seated person (code review, 1-on-1) reads as dominance. It gets compliance, not honesty — you'll hear "okay, fine" instead of the real opinion.
3. **Respect in Japanese business culture.** Japan is a **non-contact, wider-distance culture** — preferences for personal distance are consistently larger than in Western cultures, and physical touch (handshakes, pats on the back) is essentially absent in professional settings. The bow *replaces* the handshake; distance signals respect.
4. **Reading the room includes reading distances.** Who sits where, who leans in, who pulls back — this is the map of your team's trust.
5. **Video calls have distance too.** Camera framing is your digital personal space — fill the frame and you're shouting; shrink away and you disappear.

## 3. Psychology / evidence

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| Hall's four zones (intimate/personal/social/public) | **Classic framework — reasonable practical advice.** A useful map, not measured physics; zones shift per person and culture |
| Personal space invasion triggers a stress/defense response | **Well-supported** (fight-or-flight activation, discomfort measures) |
| Japanese culture prefers larger interpersonal distance; non-contact culture | **Well-supported** (cross-cultural proxemics studies; as a *tendency* — individuals vary) |
| Hovering over a seated person reads as dominant/pressuring | **Reasonable practical advice** (ethology-informed: height + proximity = threat signals) |
| People step back under stress or when uncomfortable | **Well-supported** (a reliable discomfort cue) |
| "Standing close makes you look confident and powerful" | **Wrong for close relationships** — it reads aggressive; power shows in *choosing* distance calmly, not in shrinking it |
| "Touching someone's shoulder builds rapport at work" | **Popular claim — not for JP business culture.** Physical touch in Japanese workplaces is at best unusual, often uncomfortable. Don't |

**Culture — critical for your context (Japanese enterprise):** distance IS politeness. Keep a respectful gap with managers and clients — don't lean over desks, don't touch, don't crowd at the elevator or the printer. Bowing handles the greeting at distance. With your own team (especially non-Japanese members), normal 1-on-1 distance is fine — but *default wider with anyone senior or unfamiliar*, and always let the other person set the pace of closeness.

## 4. Common mistakes

| Mistake | What it does | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Hovering/leaning over a seated teammate's desk or screen | Pressure, dominance; they agree to make you leave | Sit down, or stand at a full arm's length |
| Standing inside the personal zone during a quick chat | Discomfort; shorter answers, less honesty | The two-step default |
| Moving closer when the other person steps back | Chasing the bubble — escalates the alarm | Stop, hold position, or step back |
| Physical touch at work (JP enterprise) | Deep discomfort, loss of trust | No touch — the bow/nod handles warmth at distance |
| Backing someone into a corner in a discussion | Threat response; they stop listening | Give them an exit — widen your stance/stay back |
| On video: face filling the whole frame | Digital space invasion — aggressive | Torso + head visible; a fist of space above your head |
| On video: tiny figure far from camera | Weak, distant, uncommitted | Camera at eye level, ~50–75 cm away, torso visible |

## 5. Workplace examples

- **1-on-1 with a team member:** sit at the same level (both sitting), roughly an arm's length+ apart, maybe at a 90° angle (side-corner of a table) rather than face-on — the angle reads less confronting.
- **Code review of a struggling member:** pull up a chair and sit beside them, rather than standing over their shoulder. Same eye level, same distance, different power.
- **Giving bad news to a manager (JP context):** keep a respectful distance, straight posture, slight bow at the greeting; do not approach the desk or crowd the seat.
- **Meeting room:** when you're the lead, sit in a position where you can see everyone and they can see you — the head of the table or the projector side. Don't sit directly opposite a single person at a close narrow table (that's confrontation distance).
- **Elevator / printer / corridor (JP):** keep space; a small nod is the greeting. Don't make small talk from inside someone's personal bubble.
- **Video call:** set the camera at eye level, ~50–75 cm away, so your head *and* shoulders are visible with a fist of space above your head. When someone joins, don't lean into the screen.
- **Interview:** sit back comfortably in the chair (posture lesson), keep two-steps-ish distance, let the interviewer set any closer distance. Never lean across the desk.

## 6. Before vs After

| 😟 Nervous version | 😌 Calm / confident version |
|---|---|
| Stands over the teammate's desk while reviewing | Sits beside them, same eye level |
| Leans in when the other person leans back | Holds position, gives space |
| Fills the whole camera frame | Torso + head, eye-level camera |
| Crowds the manager's desk to talk (JP) | Bows at a respectful distance |
| Touches shoulders/arms to build rapport | Warm words + nod; no touch |
| Opposite face-on at a narrow table | Side-angle seating, comfortable gap |

## 7. Practical drills (measurable)

1. **Know your arm's length — 2 min.** Stand with one arm extended: fingertips mark roughly the start of the social zone. Now step back one more small step — commit this distance to muscle memory (it's your meeting default).
2. **The approach drill — 5 min, with a willing friend.** Approach them slowly from 3 m; ask them to raise one finger the moment they feel slightly uncomfortable. Stop, note the distance, step back half a step. Repeat 5 times. You'll learn that their alarm goes off *before* you think it does.
3. **The lean-in reading — all day, passive.** In every conversation today, watch where the other person's *feet* point and whether they lean in or back. Count how many times you can predict the direction of the conversation from distance alone.
4. **The chair drill — 3 min.** Next 1-on-1: choose the side-angle seat (90° corner) on purpose. Note how the conversation feels vs face-on seating.
5. **The camera frame drill — 10 min.** Set your camera at eye level, ~50–75 cm away. Record 90 seconds: your head AND torso visible, a fist of space above your head, face not filling the frame. Replay and adjust until the framing is right; save it as your position.
6. **The stop drill — 1 min × 3.** Walk toward a colleague's desk and stop at the default distance instead of leaning in. Feel the habit of *approaching* — then practice *stopping*.
