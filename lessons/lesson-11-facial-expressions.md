# Lesson 11 — Facial Expressions

> Program: Leadership Presence & Body Language · Phase 3 — Communication Control (3/4)
> Delivered: 2026-09-20 · Builds on: Authentic Smile (L6), Eye Contact (L5), Calm Voice (L3)

---

## 1. Core concept

**Your face is the most-read surface in the room — and it must agree with your message.** The single rule of facial presence is **congruence**: your face repeats what your words say, and never contradicts them. Good news needs a face that carries warmth. Difficult news needs a face that carries weight. Under pressure, a leader's face shows *calm focus* — not panic, and not a blank mask.

**Mental model:** your face is a **replay of your words**. When people watch you, they play two channels at once — your voice and your face — and when the two disagree, they always believe the face. A smile during bad news says "this isn't serious." A stone face during praise says "I don't mean it." The face never loses the argument against the words — so make them say the same thing.

Three faces you should master:

1. **The neutral face** — calm, relaxed, attentive. This is your *default at work*: not smiling, not frowning — a pleasant, open, still face. In Japanese business culture this neutral face is professional, not cold.
2. **The warm face** — the genuine smile (L6) when something is actually good. Used as *punctuation*, at real moments of warmth — never as a default mask.
3. **The serious face** — calm, slightly stiller, softer than usual, with steady eyes — for difficult news, conflicts, and decisions. Serious does NOT mean angry: no frown, no hard stare.

## 2. Why it matters

1. **Credibility is lost in the mismatch.** If your face contradicts your words even once (a smile while describing a production risk), people notice — and from then on they trust the words less.
2. **The calm face under pressure is your authority.** In an incident, the face that stays calm-focus while everyone else shows panic is the face of the leader — the same anchor effect as L8, on your face.
3. **It makes you approachable.** A junior developer deciding whether to ask you a question reads your face first. Warm, open = "I can ask." Neutral-pleasant = "maybe." Frowning-in-thought = "don't bother me."
4. **It works across the room and on camera.** On video, your face fills the frame — a tiny expression is twice as visible. For a remote team, your face IS most of your presence.
5. **It's your honesty amplifier.** Genuine reactions are hard to fake — so you don't fake them. When your face really shows what you feel *in a controlled way*, people trust both the face and the person.

## 3. Psychology / evidence

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| Basic emotions are recognized on faces across cultures | **Well-supported** (Ekman's basic emotions), with a big caveat: *display rules* differ — cultures decide which faces are shown publicly |
| People can tell a genuine smile from a fake one | **Well-supported** (the Duchenne smile — real smiles involve the eyes; see L6) |
| The face carries more weight than the voice when they conflict | **Reasonable practical advice** + some support (nonverbal channel dominates when channels disagree) |
| "Micro-expressions let you detect lies" (the 'Lie to Me' idea) | **Popular claim — limited evidence** for practical lie detection. Don't build habits on this. |
| A neutral face at work means "angry" or "cold" | **Wrong for your context** — in Japanese business culture a neutral, calm face is the professional default; reading it as anger is a Western assumption |
| Panic on the face spreads to others (fear contagion) | **Some support** (emotional contagion is real; your face sets the room's temperature) |

**Culture — critical for your context (Japanese enterprise):** Japanese display rules favor *masking strong emotions* — especially negatives — in public. A neutral, composed face in a serious meeting is a sign of professionalism and self-control, NOT a signal that something is wrong. Two cautions: (1) don't over-smile at managers or clients — the Western "always-smile" default can read as unserious or unreadable in Japan; match the room's register. (2) When your face goes neutral with a Japanese colleague, they don't need you to "fix" it into a smile — calm neutrality is the respectful state.

## 4. Common mistakes

| Mistake | What it does | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Smiling while delivering bad news / risks | Contradicts the words; erodes trust in everything you say | The serious face: calm, stiller, steady eyes — let the words carry the weight |
| Stone face while someone praises your team | Praise bounces off you — demotivating, reads as ungrateful | The warm face at genuine moments: real smile + nod (L6) |
| The "thinking frown" — squinting/frowning while concentrating | Looks angry or disapproving to everyone else in the meeting | Deliberately relax the brow when you're listening/thinking in view of others |
| Faking reactions (big surprise/laughter you don't feel) | Reads as fake — people sense it instantly | React in proportion; a small genuine nod beats a big fake laugh |
| Frozen mask on video (the "translator face") | Makes you look disengaged or robotic to remote teammates | Record yourself, watch muted (drill below), let natural micro-reactions show |
| Neutral-but-hard (no softening at all) | Feels cold, unapproachable — juniors won't come to you | The pleasant neutral: relaxed brow, soft eyes, occasional small nod |

## 5. Workplace examples

- **Incident call:** inside you're nervous, but your face shows *calm focus* — relaxed brow, steady eyes, no darting. Teammates mirror your face before they mirror your words (this is L8's anchor, running on your face). Panic on your face spreads; calm on your face calms the room.
- **Code review disagreement:** you disagree, but your face stays *open* — no sneer, no hardening. Then you speak. The open face is what keeps it a discussion instead of a fight.
- **Praising a member for a great fix:** this is a genuine moment — let the warm face show (real smile + a nod). The gratitude lands *because* the face carries it. Words alone feel flat.
- **Receiving criticism from your manager:** neutral-attentive face (relaxed brow, steady eyes, small nods) — not defensive frown, not fake smile. Your face says: I'm listening; criticism isn't a threat.
- **Interview (video):** pleasant attentive face; smile at genuinely warm moments (the interviewer's joke that's actually funny); serious face for the "why did you leave" question. The interviewer reads your congruence the whole time.
- **Japanese context:** serious scope meeting → calm neutral face is correct. Manager shares a deadline problem → no dramatic reaction; a composed, slightly stiller face, then a structured response. Your face says: this is weighty, and I can handle weight.

## 7. Practical drills (measurable)

1. **The mute replay — record 5 min and watch SILENT.** Answer 3 questions on camera. Replay with sound OFF. Does your face tell the same story your words told? Mark each moment the face and content disagreed — that's your congruence gap.
2. **The three-faces drill — mirror, 2 min each.** Practise the three faces in a mirror: neutral (relaxed brow, soft eyes — check: does it look calm or angry?), warm (genuine smile, eyes involved — L6), serious (calm, still, no frown). Cycle 3 times. Learn what each feels like internally so you can call it up.
3. **The thinking-face check — one meeting.** Every time you listen/concentrate in a meeting, consciously feel your brow. Count how many times you catch yourself frowning while someone else talks. Release it: relax the brow, keep the eyes soft.
4. **The bad-news face — 2 min (record).** Deliver a fake-but-real message to camera: "The migration failed and we may lose a day." Replay muted: is your face congruent with the words (serious, calm) or contradicting them (smiling, shrugging)?
5. **The praise drill — one real moment today.** When someone does good work, deliver genuine praise WITH the warm face + a nod — not just words. Practice feeling glad before speaking, so the face is real.
6. **The spot-the-mismatch drill — 10 min.** Watch a recorded meeting or interview (TED, dev Q&A). Mute it and read faces only: note every moment the speaker's face contradicts the situation (a manager smiling while announcing layoffs is the classic). Once you see it in others, you'll feel it in yourself.

## 10. Coaching exercise — ✋ STOP HERE

Two parts:

1. **The two-messages test (record):** on camera, deliver: (1) good news — "the feature is done and tested, nice work everyone," then (2) difficult news — "the rollback is failing and we might lose the day's data." Replay with SOUND OFF. Was the face congruent for both? Where did it leak (a smile on the bad news? a flat face on the good)?
2. **The real-life congruence watch (today):** in one real conversation, deliberately run the serious-face + warm-face at the right moments, and catch one thinking-frown.

Then answer **4 questions**:

1. Which face is your *default* under stress — and does it leak into moments where it shouldn't?
2. In the recording — what was the strongest moment of congruence, and the biggest mismatch?
3. Do you tend to over-smile (to be liked) or under-react (too flat)? Which one is your risk?
4. Rate your **facial congruence** 1–5.
