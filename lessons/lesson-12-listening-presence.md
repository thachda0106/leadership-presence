# Lesson 12 — Listening Presence

> Program: Leadership Presence & Body Language · Phase 3 — Communication Control (4/4)
> Delivered: 2026-09-20 · Builds on: Eye Contact (L5), Silent (L10), Mirroring (L8), Facial Expressions (L11)

---

## 1. Core concept

**Listening has a body — and it is the highest-status move in a conversation.** Presence in listening is the whole package: you give your full attention, your body says "I am here, all of me," and you prove you heard by *reflecting back* what was said. Most people in meetings are not listening — they are waiting for their turn to talk. A leader is the one person who genuinely receives, and it is that person others most want to talk to.

**Mental model:** listening is **catching a ball before throwing one**. Most people start throwing their reply while the other person's ball is still in the air — they catch nothing, and the conversation becomes two people throwing past each other. The listener-with-presence *receives fully first*: eyes in (L5), body open (L1), brief nod (L8), face reacting (L11) — and only then, with the ball safely caught, returns it: a paraphrase ("so what you're saying is…") and a real follow-up question.

The three **verbal receipts** — the proof that you heard:

1. **The paraphrase** — restate their load-bearing point in your words: *"so the risk is the rollback timing, not the deploy itself."* Use it on important points only — paraphrasing everything sounds robotic.
2. **The follow-up question** — ask about the thing they *just* said, not your own agenda: *"what makes you say the rollback is the risky part?"* A good follow-up is the loudest proof of listening.
3. **The window silence** — let them finish, let their last words sit 2–3 seconds (L10), only then respond.

## 2. Why it matters

1. **Trust is built in the listening.** People don't trust those who talk well; they trust those who *hear them*. A leader who listens is a leader people open up to — which is how you learn the real problems before they explode (a teammate's "I'm fine" hiding a burnout, an incident detail everyone glossed over).
2. **You get better information.** You can't fix what you don't fully understand. The fix-it reflex (jumping to solutions) is the #1 enemy of real listening — the pause and the paraphrase are its cure.
3. **It's your quietest power move.** In a meeting where everyone competes to speak, the person who listens, reflects, and then speaks briefly controls the room's direction. The person who talks the most is rarely the one actually leading.
4. **It closes Phase 3.** Communication control isn't only about *your* output — it's about the whole exchange. Face (L11), silence (L10), eyes (L5), mirroring (L8): listening presence is where all of Phase 3 comes together.
5. **It fits Japanese leadership culture perfectly.** In a harmonizing culture, the person who listens, lets the senior finish, and reflects back respect is demonstrating exactly the self-control that leadership requires.

## 3. Psychology / evidence

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| People feel more liked and understood when the listener paraphrases and asks follow-ups | **Reasonable support** (active-listening research; effects are real but modest — don't over-paraphrase) |
| Multitasking while someone speaks lowers comprehension and trust | **Well-supported** (divided attention measurably hurts comprehension; the speaker notices) |
| Nodding and backchanneling ("mm-hm") make the speaker talk more fluently and openly | **Some support** — but over-nodding reads as fake (see mistakes) |
| "A good listener stays silent the whole time" | **Wrong** — passive silence is not listening. Listening is *active*: receipts (paraphrase, question) prove it |
| "Paraphrase everything you hear" | **Wrong** — mechanical echoing feels robotic and patronizing. Restate only the load-bearing points |
| In Japanese communication, listening + silence + letting the senior finish = respect | **Well-documented cultural observation** — harmony (wa) is built on attentive listening |

## 4. Common mistakes

| Mistake | What it does | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Planning your reply while they talk | You hear half, answer the wrong thing, look like you weren't there | Receive fully first: eyes in, brief nod, THEN form the reply |
| The fix-it reflex (jumping to solutions) | Cuts the speaker off; misses the real problem; patronizing | Ask one more question or paraphrase before any solution |
| Finishing their sentences | Rude everywhere; extra rude with a senior in JP | The window silence (L10): let their last words land, then speak |
| Bobblehead nodding — nodding at everything | Reads as fake agreement, not agreement with what? | Nod at the load-bearing moments only (L8: 2-second echo) |
| Phone / laptop glance mid-conversation | Loudest way to say "you are not important" | Phone down, eyes in — full presence for the person in front of you |
| Empty receipts — "I hear you" with nothing after it | Feels scripted after the second time (L8's chunk is a *start*, not a whole answer) | "I hear you — tell me more about X" / paraphrase the point |
| Asking a question you already know the answer to | Proves you weren't listening (the famous trap) | Ask only about what they *just* contributed |

## 5. Workplace examples

- **1-on-1:** your member explains a deployment fear in a rambling way. You receive fully (no planning), then: "so the real worry is the rollback — if the migration breaks, we're stuck. What have you tried so far?" The paraphrase organizes THEIR thinking; the follow-up moves it forward.
- **Stand-up:** someone gives a dense update. Instead of rushing "ok, next," ask one real follow-up: "the cache issue — is it the invalidation or the TTL?" That single question makes the whole team pay attention.
- **Incident call:** everyone is shouting fixes. You listen to the full symptom description, then: "so the failures started after the deploy, and only for EU traffic — is that right?" The team's chaos gets organized by *your listening*.
- **Code review:** the reviewer objects. You hear the full objection (no defense yet), paraphrase it: "so your concern is that retries multiply under load, not the code itself." They feel heard → the discussion stays collaborative (this is L10 + L11 + active listening together).
- **Interview:** the interviewer's question hides a concern ("tell me about a conflict with a teammate" = "are you difficult?"). Listen for the concern underneath, reflect it, then address it: "I understand the question is really about how I handle disagreement — let me give you a concrete example."
- **Japanese context:** a manager speaks and trails off, considering. You do NOT fill it (L10). Wait, small nod, then just: "I understand — shall I summarize what we agreed?" Letting the senior's flow be respected is listening presence in JP culture.

## 6. Before vs After

| 😟 Nervous version | 😌 Calm / confident version |
|---|---|
| Plans the reply while the other person talks | Receives fully first — eyes in, body open |
| Jumps in with a solution before they finish | Window silence, then a paraphrase or a question |
| Nods at everything (bobblehead) or nothing | Nods at the load-bearing moments |
| Glances at phone / laptop mid-conversation | Full presence — the person has your whole attention |
| "Ok, next" at stand-ups | One real follow-up question per update |
| "I hear you" with nothing after it | "I hear you — and here's what I understand, is that right?" |

## 7. Practical drills (measurable)

1. **The receipts drill — one meeting.** In every conversation today, before you respond, mentally do: receive → (optional) paraphrase → (then) respond. Target: at least 3 paraphrases and 3 follow-up questions in real conversations. Count them.
2. **The 60/40 drill — one conversation.** Goal: the other person talks about 60% of the time, you about 40%. If you catch yourself over 50%, it's a signal you're not listening — you're performing.
3. **The no-fix-it drill — with a friend.** Your friend describes a genuine problem (work or personal). Your job: respond 3 times in a row WITHOUT offering a solution — only paraphrase and questions. It will feel unnatural. That's the drill.
4. **The concern-hunter drill — 5 min.** Take a hard interview question ("tell me about a time you failed") and write what concern you think lies UNDER it ("will they be honest? do they learn? do they blame others?"). Practise hearing the concern under the words — then answer the concern, not just the question.
5. **The phone-down drill — every 1-on-1 today.** Phone face-down, laptop closed if possible. Full presence, nothing competing. Notice how much more the other person says.
6. **The mutual-comprehension drill — one day.** After every substantive chat, say out loud: "so, to confirm — X, right?" One sentence. Doing it aloud trains the paraphrase reflex until it's automatic.

## 10. Coaching exercise — ✋ STOP HERE

Two parts:

1. **The listening interview (record optional):** ask a teammate or friend about "the most frustrating part of your work lately." Practice FULL listening: no advice, no fixing, only paraphrase + follow-ups. Let them talk at least 3 minutes. If you record, replay and count how many times you interrupted or offered a solution.
2. **The receipts audit (today):** in the day's conversations, count (a) how many times you paraphrased, (b) how many times you asked a real follow-up, (c) how many times you caught yourself planning a reply instead of listening.

Then answer **4 questions**:

1. When you resisted fixing and listened instead — did the other person tell you MORE than you expected?
2. Which receipt is hardest for you: the paraphrase, the follow-up question, or the silence before responding?
3. How many times did you catch yourself *planning your reply* instead of listening today?
4. Rate your **listening presence** 1–5.

## 🗣️ Today's English chunk — "Tell me more about that."

> The single best listening line in English. It does three things at once: proves you were listening, invites the other person to go deeper, and keeps you out of the fix-it reflex. Works in 1-on-1s, stand-ups, and interviews alike.

| Situation | You say |
|---|---|
| Member starts to explain a fear | "Tell me more about that — what exactly worries you?" |
| Review has a vague objection | "Tell me more about that — which part feels risky to you?" |
| Interviewer mentions a technical area | "Tell me more about that — how does your team use it?" |

Your turn: say *"That's interesting — tell me more about ____."*
